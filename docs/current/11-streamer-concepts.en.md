The user requested ideas that may completely depart from the existing concept, across cooperation, competition and combat, with entertaining random situations for streams. This does not constitute a decision to replace the delivery game in the [overview](01-overview.en.md). All player counts, timings and rules below are proposals.

## Criteria and references

Stream suitability hypothesis: the objective should be easy to understand, accident causes should read on screen, and player choices should amplify or resolve incidents. Randomness presents situations; people choose responses. Avoid prolonged elimination and arbitrary instant deaths that only change outcomes. Friends should be able to start while using voice chat; viewer voting or platform integration is optional.

Official descriptions present [PEAK](https://landfall.se/peak) as cooperative climbing where small mistakes are dangerous, [R.E.P.O.](https://store.steampowered.com/app/3241660/REPO/) as physics-based valuable-object extraction, and [ROUNDS](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1557740/ROUNDS/) as combat where the loser selects upgrades to counter the opponent. Checked 2026-09-10. These are mechanical references, not streaming popularity statistics or evidence that the proposed ideas will succeed.

## 1. House Rules

2–4-player competition. Start with a simple game of putting one ball into your own goal. Between rounds, choose one of 3 random rule candidates to apply to everyone. The ball becomes hot, jumping attracts it, or goals walk around. Keep at most 3 active rules and replace the oldest.

Scene: a player selects magnetic jumping to stop the leader but accidentally delivers the ball from their own goal to the opponent. A choice backfiring creates accountability and arguments. Random candidates should still offer viable responses. Risk: combinations become unreadable or make victory impossible. First experiment: 1 arena, 6 rules and scoring.

## 2. Defective Heroes

2–4-player cooperative combat. Between fights, select abilities with benefits and side effects from random candidates. Strong healing knocks its target back, shields attract enemies, and revival causes an explosion. Preserve normal controls and visibly telegraph side effects.

Scene: healing knocks a friend off a cliff, but the same knockback later launches them across a gap. An accident-causing ability also becoming a solution is the distinction. Risk: repeated friendly interference becomes exhausting. First experiment: 1 room, 2 enemy types, 6 abilities and quick rescue.

## 3. Mech Driving Test

3–4-player cooperation. Split movement, arms, weapons and power on one robot while completing obstacle and combat tests. Random faults cause overheating, locked arms and component swaps. Announce control reassignment and perform it between test sections.

Scene: the driver reverses just as the arm operator throws a captured enemy into a friendly generator. Coordination conversations become the entertainment. Risk: some roles lack activity or the screen becomes unreadable. First experiment: 1 robot, 1 test ground and continuing responsibilities for each role.

## 4. Cursed Auction

3–6-player competition and negotiation. Bid on objects using limited appraisal information, then carry purchases home to cash out. Curses are random but inferable from clues. A safe becomes heavier as it consumes money; a crown attracts nearby people.

Scene: a bargain crown pulls the buyer's competitors along as the buyer runs. Auction bravado connects to physical consequences. Risk: insufficient information makes bidding feel like pure gambling. First experiment: an auction room and return corridor, 6 objects and identifiable clues for each.

## 5. Weird Laws

2–4-player cooperative stealth. Steal objects and escape while each area enforces random laws: no red items, no jumping near guards, or entry only in pairs. Entrance signs and violation warnings reveal the laws; overlap at most 2 high-impact laws.

Scene: hide a friend carrying a red gem in a box to cross a checkpoint, then discover the box is also red. Remembering rules and finding loopholes drive play. Risk: it becomes a test of memorizing lengthy text. First experiment: 3 rooms, 1 guard type, 5 laws and clear warnings.

## 6. Boss by Committee

3–4-player asymmetric combat. One player controls the boss and the others play heroes; rotate the boss after short matches. The boss assembles a body from random part candidates. Powerful parts have visible weaknesses: a huge hammer sticks in the floor; long legs are fast but easy to trip.

Scene: extended arms become a climbing route for heroes to reach the boss's head. Opponents exploit the builder's own choices. Risk: animation, collision and asymmetric balancing costs. First experiment: 1 body, 3 interchangeable parts and basic hero attacks.

## 7. Disaster Realty

2–4-player competition with temporary cooperation. Build houses on a small island and survive random disasters to retain the most house value. Forecast the next disaster category so players can prepare, then vary intensity and direction. Floods, strong winds and giant birds demand different construction choices.

Scene: a player raises their house against floods, then a wind forecast makes them ask the neighbor they just mocked for permission to tether their homes together. Cooperation and rivalry shift with circumstances. Risk: networked building physics and compounding leader advantages. First experiment: a small lot, 3 block types and 2 disasters, with basic materials replenished after each disaster.

## 8. Wish Carefully

2–4-player cooperative roguelite. Clear rooms and choose random wish candidates with foreseeable costs. Bigger weapons jam in doors, faster movement takes longer to brake, and wealth adds coin weight. Use authored wish combinations; free-text AI adjudication is unnecessary.

Scene: a friend wishes for a larger weapon and cannot fit through a door, so teammates break a wall to make a route. Shared greed and dealing with consequences drive play. Risk: drawbacks become one-off jokes. First experiment: 3 rooms, 6 wishes and two uses for each drawback.

## Priorities and proposed validation

- First experiment: idea 1. A simple ball-and-goal objective supports testing choices and reversals with relatively few rules.
- Cooperative streams: idea 2. Aim for messing up, rescuing friends and discovering combinations. Combat feel and friendly interference need careful tuning.
- Strong visual identity: idea 3. Multiple players controlling one body is legible. Role enjoyment and robot presentation cost more to resolve.

All candidates target brief initial explanations, short rounds or sections, and quick re-entry. Proposed timing is 3–5 minutes per section and 15–25 minutes per session. Player ranges are design proposals, not guarantees from existing networking functionality.

After selection, initial human testing should check whether players read the objective without explanation, describe failure causes in their own words, respond differently to the same random incident, avoid long elimination waits, and voluntarily replay. Fun should work without viewers first. No implementation, human play or streaming validation was performed in this task.
